Overview

ARGUMENT: Courts in France, the United States, and China are not neutral arbiters — they are active architects of linguistic hegemony, consistently privileging majority languages while foreclosing meaningful minority-language protections.

Theoretical Framework

Imagined Communities

— Anderson (2006)

Shared language enables ‘imagined’ national solidarity through print capitalism and bureaucratic standardization

Linguistic Capital

— Bourdieu (1997, 1987, 2001)

Language carries symbolic power — institutions convert speech varieties into markers of social distinction with material consequences

Hegemonic Preservation

— Hirschl (2004, 2007)

Elites empower courts when their status is threatened; judicialization becomes a tool of cultural hegemony

Linguistic Imperialism

— Phillipson (1992)

Domestic language hierarchies interact with international structures that reward Anglophone states

→ Courts sit at the intersection of all four frameworks, translating elite anxiety into linguistic hierarchy

Three Judicial Modalities

FRANCE

Entrenchment

Courts actively interpret law to expand dominant-language protections and reject minority claims on constitutional grounds

UNITED STATES

Restraint

Courts avoid developing language-rights doctrine through narrow statutory interpretation and jurisdictional avoidance

CHINA

Foreclosure

Courts systematically refuse to register or adjudicate minority language claims, treating them as non-justiciable

Judicial Entrenchment in Practice

Constitutional architecture: Article 2 joins French national identity inseparably to the French language; Article 75-1 offers symbolic ‘heritage’ protection for regional languages without justiciable rights.
Cour de cassation (1986): Refused to limit language law to consumer protection — instead defended ‘Frenchness’ itself
Conseil constitutionnel (1994 & 2021): Entrenched French as a constitutional imperative while gutting legislative attempts to protect regional languages (Loi Toubon, Loi Molac).
Root cause: ‘Grandeur française’ — elite anxiety about French’s declining global status drives judicial actors to use constitutional law as the last defense of linguistic prestige.

Restraint by Default

Constitutional silence: The Founders never enshrined English, yet that silence has enabled English dominance to go constitutionally unchallenged for over two centuries.
Statutory over constitutional: Lau’s reliance on the Civil Rights Act rather than the Equal Protection Clause made language protections fragile; Sandoval later stripped private enforcement.
Avoidance in Arizonans: The Court had a clear opportunity to rule on Official English laws and refused, leaving the door open for state-level English mandates.
Root cause: American hegemonic complacency, English is secure globally, so the Court can afford silence; that silence is not neutral but preserves the status quo.

Systematic Foreclosure

Structural non-justiciability: Minority language cases — Uyghur education in Xinjiang, Tibetan bilingual schooling — never reach adjudication; courts refuse to register them.
‘Rights’ vs. ‘Freedom’: The law frames pǔtōnghuà access as a positive right backed by the state, but minority language use as a mere ‘freedom’; a textual hierarchy with real institutional consequences.
Symbolic concession: Art. 134 guarantees minority language court access; in practice, judges waive interpreters if a defendant ‘appears’ to understand Mandarin.
Root cause: Ascending hegemonic ambition; linguistic uniformity is framed as civilizational fulfillment, a ‘dream of the Chinese nation for thousands of years.’

The International Dimension

Does supranational oversight change the picture? Or can it?

ICCPR / UNHRC
  • Art. 27 protects minority language use
  • UNHRC is ‘highly deferential’ to state practice
  • Guesdon v. France: committee avoided Art. 27 entirely
  • US: ICCPR deemed ‘not self-executing’
  • China: non-ratified signatory — minimal accountability
ECtHR (Europe only)
  • Most impactful of international bodies
  • ECHR Arts. 6 & 14: fair trial + non-discrimination
  • Compelled France to revise fair trial procedures
  • European Charter for Regional/Minority Languages
  • France has NOT ratified ECRML, stopped by Conseil constitutionnel
ICJ & Linguistic Design
  • No compulsory jurisdiction over language policy
  • States invoke sovereignty to resist constraints
  • French is a working language of ICJ & ECtHR
  • Language of the court is itself a mechanism of hierarchy

Conclusion

Language policy is fundamentally political. Courts are not neutral arbiters, they are active architects of linguistic hegemony, and their choices reflect the temporal location of their state within the global hegemonic order.
↘France (declining): Judicial entrenchment as a last institutional defense of diminishing linguistic grandeur
→USA (dominant): Restraint masks complacency born of hegemonic security, silence preserves the status quo
↗China (ascending): Foreclosure operationalizes linguistic uniformity as a prerequisite for global hegemonic rise
